The Money Talk — How to Map Your Finances After a Layoff

TLG
The Layoff Guide
April 6, 2026 · 6 min read

Okay. You made it through yesterday. You took the walk. You made the call. You let yourself feel it. Nice work — seriously.

Now let's talk about money.

I know. This is the part everyone dreads. The "how long can I survive?" conversation you've been having with the ceiling at 3am. But here's the thing: the unknown is always scarier than the known. Once we put real numbers on paper, the panic almost always goes down — because you usually have more runway than you think.

So let's do this together. Grab a notebook, open a spreadsheet, or honestly just use the back of an envelope. We're going to figure out exactly where you stand in about 20 minutes.

Step 1: The T-Chart — Income vs. Expenses

Draw a line down the middle of your page. Left side: money coming in. Right side: money going out. That's it. No fancy accounting — just an honest snapshot.

Here's what it might look like:

💰 Money Coming In

  • Severance (if any) $X,XXX
  • Unemployment benefits $XXX/wk
  • Partner's income $X,XXX
  • Side gig / freelance $XXX
  • Last paycheck + PTO payout $X,XXX

📋 Money Going Out

  • Rent / Mortgage $X,XXX
  • Car payment $XXX
  • Insurance (COBRA/marketplace) $XXX
  • Groceries $XXX
  • Utilities & phone $XXX
  • Subscriptions $XXX
  • Minimum debt payments $XXX

Don't overthink it. Rough numbers are fine. We're going for "directionally correct," not "auditor-approved." Check your bank statement from last month if you need help — most banks let you download a spending summary.

Step 2: The Subscription Purge

You know all those $9.99 and $14.99 charges that quietly drain your account every month? Now's the time to go through every single one and ask: do I actually need this right now?

Quick wins — things you can pause or cancel today:

Streaming services you barely use (keep one) ~$30-50/mo
Gym membership (most allow a "freeze") ~$30-80/mo
Premium app subscriptions (Spotify, cloud storage, etc.) ~$15-30/mo
Meal kit deliveries ~$60-120/mo
Subscription boxes ~$20-50/mo
Unused SaaS tools or professional memberships ~$10-50/mo

Those "small" charges add up fast. Most people find $100-300/month in subscriptions they can pause without really noticing. That's real money — that's groceries, that's a car payment, that's an extra month of runway.

Step 3: Calculate Your Runway

This is the big one. This is the number that will either keep you up at night or help you sleep. Let's make it the second one.

Your Runway Formula
Total Savings ÷ Monthly Expenses = Months of Runway
Example: $12,000 savings ÷ $3,200/month = 3.75 months

Now add in any income that's coming — severance, unemployment benefits, a partner's paycheck, freelance work. That extends your runway further. Most people, once they actually do this math, realize they're not in "the house is on fire" territory. They're in "I have some time to be thoughtful about this" territory. And that is a very different feeling.

"Knowing your numbers doesn't create the problem — it takes away the panic. You probably have more runway than you think."

Step 4: Don't Forget the Income Side

Here's something people forget when they're panicking about expenses: there's money coming your way too.

Unemployment benefits. If you were laid off (not fired for cause), you almost certainly qualify. In most states, benefits replace 40-50% of your previous income, up to a cap. It's not everything, but it's not nothing. Find your state's unemployment page here →

Your last paycheck and PTO. Most states require your employer to pay out unused vacation and PTO. Check your state's labor laws — in many cases, that check is bigger than you expect.

Severance. If it was offered, great. If it wasn't, it doesn't hurt to ask — especially if you're being asked to sign a release. That's a negotiation, not a formality.

SNAP and food assistance. I'm going to say this plainly: if you qualify, use it. These programs exist precisely for moments like this. There is no shame in it. It's a bridge, not a destination. Check available assistance programs here →

Twenty minutes with a pen and paper can change everything.

Step 5: Put It All in One Place

Now take everything — the income, the expenses, the subscriptions you're cutting, the runway number — and put it in one document you can look at whenever the 3am panic hits. It doesn't have to be fancy.

Here's the thing about this exercise: it's not fun. Nobody wants to sit down and stare at their bills. But the moment you do it — the moment you replace "I have no idea how long I can survive" with "I have 4.2 months of runway and here's how I can stretch it to 6" — the entire game changes. You go from reactive to proactive. From panicking to planning.

One More Thing: No Shame in This Game

If your savings are thin, that's okay. If your runway is shorter than you'd like, that's okay. If you're looking at the numbers thinking "this is tight," that's okay too. You're not behind. You're not bad with money. You just got hit with something unexpected, and now you're dealing with it — which is exactly the right thing to do.

The people who struggle most after a layoff aren't the ones with small savings accounts. They're the ones who avoid looking at the numbers entirely. You're not doing that. You're here, doing the work. That puts you ahead of most people already.

"You don't need a perfect financial plan. You need a 20-minute honest look at your numbers. That's enough to start."
